Is "Surplus Land" Really Surplus?

Presenting Russian Imperial Land Surveys
on the Kazakh Steppe in the Early Twentieth Century

Kazakhstan

Project Overview

This project examines a large-scale Russian imperial survey of Kazakh pastoral communities in the early twentieth century. It was likely the first—and perhaps the last—investigation of this scale conducted among Kazakh communities before later political transformations reshaped the region.

The survey emerged from the pressures of Russian imperial expansion and peasant migration. After the emancipation of Russian serfs and the expansion of transportation networks such as the Siberian Railway, the Kazakh steppe became an important target for agricultural settlement. Imperial officials used land surveys to identify so-called “surplus land” that could be transferred to Russian peasant settlers.

Using this survey as its historical basis, this project asks whether “surplus land” truly existed. Did Russian imperial officials and Kazakh communities understand land use in the same way? Was “surplus land” land that could not be used, or land that colonial conditions made difficult for Kazakh communities to use?

Why It Matters

This project argues that the survey was not only a technical instrument of colonial land appropriation. It also recorded a deeper encounter between two different systems of land use: sedentary agriculture and mobile pastoralism.

Russian imperial surveyors often evaluated Kazakh land through agricultural assumptions, while Kazakh communities depended on seasonal movement, pasture rotation, livestock capacity, and access to different ecological zones. The category of “surplus land” therefore reveals not only colonial expansion, but also a misunderstanding of pastoral systems.

Although this encounter took place more than a century ago, misunderstandings of nomadic land use continue today. By reconstructing how imperial officials measured and categorized Kazakh pastoral space, this project shows why the differences between agricultural and pastoral systems still matter for understanding nomadic societies.
