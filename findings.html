Findings: Was “Surplus Land” Really Surplus?

This page brings together the main argument of the project. It argues that “surplus land” was not simply unused or empty land on the Kazakh steppe. Instead, the survey produced “surplus land” through its purpose, method, and final redistribution of land.

1

From the Purpose of the Survey

The survey’s purpose was not simply to describe the Kazakh steppe. Its deeper goal was to identify land that could be made available for Russian peasant settlement. The thirty district descriptions show that officials repeatedly evaluated land according to whether it was suitable for agriculture, cultivation, and settlement.

In this sense, the survey translated pastoral landscapes into agricultural categories. Land was not only observed; it was assessed through the needs of future settlers.

2

From the Method of Calculation

The survey’s method also shaped the production of “surplus land.” Officials based their calculations on households that were considered to be making full use of land. However, the survey itself suggested that nearly 90 percent of households in the region were poor. This means that the model of “full use” mainly reflected wealthier households with sufficient livestock.

For poorer households, land that appeared unused was not necessarily idle. It could be land they were unable to use because they lacked livestock. In a pastoral system, access to land depended on the possession of animals: without livestock, a household could not fully enter or use pastureland. If this unused land was classified as “surplus,” then poor Kazakh households permanently lost access to land that they might have needed in the future.

From the Final Result and Local Responses

Local Kazakh responses show that the land assigned to Russian settlers was not simply abandoned or useless land. Instead, Kazakh writers and readers’ letters repeatedly described the loss of good land, especially land that was most suitable for cultivation and settlement.

These responses challenge the official language of “surplus land.” From the perspective of local Kazakh communities, the land transferred to Russian peasants was often valuable land, not land without use.

Conclusion

Taken together, the survey’s purpose, method, and results suggest that the real function of the investigation was to justify the transfer of land to Russian peasants. “Surplus land” was not discovered as a neutral fact. Rather, it was produced through imperial categories, calculations, and assumptions about agricultural settlement.

The survey made Kazakh pastoral land appear available because it measured the steppe through the needs of Russian colonization rather than through the logic of Kazakh pastoral life. Therefore, the problem was not that the Kazakh steppe naturally contained unused surplus land. The problem was that imperial survey methods redefined pastoral land as land available for settlement.
